Three kinds of phonetic signs

Every hieroglyph used for its sound (rather than as a picture-word) spells either one, two, or three consonants. Egyptologists call these uniliteral, biliteral, and triliteral signs.

Uniliteral signs spell a single consonant — there are about 24 of them, and they function like an alphabet. The word rn ("name") is spelled with two of them back to back:

rn — name
Mouth sign (r) + water-ripple sign (n) — two uniliterals, each spelling exactly one sound, placed side by side to build the word.

Biliteral signs spell a fixed pair of consonants in one picture. There are a few dozen of these — you have to learn each one's sound the way you'd learn a new alphabet letter, since the picture doesn't tell you the sound. sn ("brother") uses one:

sn — brother
The arrow sign alone spells s-n — both consonants in a single sign, followed by the seated-man determinative.

Triliteral signs spell three consonants at once. There are fewer of these, and several double as the everyday ideogram for a common word. nfr ("good, beautiful") is a single triliteral sign, nothing more:

nfr — good, beautiful
The heart-and-windpipe sign spells all three sounds n-f-r by itself.

Reading direction matches the way the signs face: in this dictionary's font, and in the crops throughout this tool, everything reads left to right — but Egyptian could just as easily be written right to left, or top to bottom in columns. Animate signs (birds, people, animals) always face toward the start of the line.

Redundant complements

Because a biliteral or triliteral sign's sound isn't spelled out letter by letter, scribes very often added one or two uniliteral signs after it that repeat part of the sound it already carries. These are called phonetic complements, and when they just restate a sound the main sign already gives you, they're redundant — there for clarity and rhythm, not because the word needs them.

A classic case is wsr ("strong, powerful") — the triliteral sign already spells all three sounds w-s-r, and yet it's followed by two more signs:

wsr — strong, powerful
Head-and-neck sign (triliteral wsr) + folded-cloth sign (s, redundant) + mouth sign (r, redundant). The complements repeat the last two consonants of the triliteral — that's the usual pattern: complements restate the end of the sign's value, not the beginning.

The same happens with biliterals. mn ("firm, established") pairs its biliteral sign with one redundant complement:

mn — to be firm, established
Draughtboard-and-piece sign (biliteral mn) + water-ripple sign (n, redundant) — repeating just the final consonant.

Not every complement is redundant, though — sometimes a sign genuinely supplies a sound the main sign doesn't have. In imn ("hidden"), the reed-leaf sign at the front supplies the i that the biliteral mn sign doesn't carry at all, so that one isn't redundant — only the water-ripple n that follows is.

Determinatives

Because vowels aren't written, many different words end up spelled with exactly the same consonants. To help sort out which word is meant, scribes added a silent sign at the very end of a word — never the beginning or middle — that isn't pronounced at all. It carries no sound; it just tells the reader what category of thing the word belongs to.

These are called determinatives. You can spot them because removing one never changes how the word is read aloud — only what it's understood to mean.

sn — brother
After the biliteral sign spelling s-n, a small seated man sits at the end — not pronounced, just flagging "this word is about a male person."
ẖr — to fall
Here the determinative is a little toppling man — it literally pictures the action the verb describes.

Determinatives are extremely common — most Middle Egyptian words that aren't grammatical particles have one. Common categories include people (by gender or role), gods, the sun/time, plants, abstract ideas, and specific actions like falling or walking.

A related but different device is the ideogram stroke — a single vertical mark placed after a sign to say "read this sign as the actual word/picture it shows, not just for its sound." You'll meet this on words like pr ("house") and ib ("heart"), where the main sign is already a recognisable picture of the thing itself.
